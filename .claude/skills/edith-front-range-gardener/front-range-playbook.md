## 1. Climate & site fundamentals

- **Hardiness:** USDA Zone 6a for the user's ZIP (avg. annual extreme min ≈ -10 to -5 °F). Denver metro overall spans roughly 5b–6a; foothills and outlying areas can be 5a or colder. Always match perennials/trees/shrubs to Zone 6 *or lower (colder)*.
- **Elevation:** ~5,280 ft in Denver, higher toward the foothills. Thin atmosphere → intense UV, faster soil drying, bigger day/night temperature swings.
- **Precipitation:** semi-arid, ~15 in/year, much of it as spring snow. Summers are dry; supplemental water is mandatory for most non-xeric plants.
- **Frost window (Front Range rule of thumb, CSU):** last spring frost ≈ **May 15**, first fall frost ≈ **Oct 15**, ≈150-day season. NOAA/almanac figures for the city of Denver proper sometimes read earlier (≈ early May / early Oct); the conservative mid-May / mid-Oct framing protects tender crops. Microclimate and elevation shift this by 1–3 weeks. **Always pair any date with "watch the actual forecast."**
- **Soil temperature matters as much as air temp** for seeding/transplanting — warm-season crops want soil ≈ 60 °F+.
- **Hazards:** "Hail Alley" (peak May–Aug — keep floating row cover ready); Chinook winds; sudden hard freezes after warm spells; winter sun + dry wind causing desiccation. Evergreens, new plantings, and lawns need **winter watering** on warm (>40 °F), dry, snow-free days, mid-day so it soaks before night.

## 2. The season: a month-by-month rhythm

Use as a scaffold; confirm specific dates/varieties against CSU PlantTalk or Extension fact sheets.

- **Jan–Feb:** Plan. Order seeds. Start onions/leeks and slow growers indoors late Feb. Winter-water on warm dry days. Prune dormant fruit trees (not spring-bloomers).
- **March:** Start tomatoes/peppers/eggplant indoors (~6–8 wks before mid-May). Direct-sow peas, spinach, radish as soil works. Cut back ornamental grasses/perennials.
- **April:** Plant cool-season crops (lettuce, brassicas, carrots, potatoes, onions). Prep beds. Plant bare-root trees/shrubs. Hold tender crops — frosts still come.
- **May:** Harden off transplants. **Plant tender crops after ~May 15 / "Mother's Day," forecast permitting.** Mulch. Row cover at the ready for late frost + hail.
- **June:** Everything's in. Establish deep, infrequent watering. Mulch to hold moisture. Watch for early hail.
- **July–Aug:** Peak heat/UV — consistent water, harvest often. Succession-sow fall cool crops in mid-late summer (count back ~weeks from Oct 15). Hail vigilance.
- **September:** Fall cool-season harvest. First frost can arrive — cover tender crops on cold nights. Great month to plant trees/shrubs/perennials (warm soil, cooling air).
- **October:** First frost ~mid-month ends warm crops. Plant spring bulbs. Garlic in. Begin winter-watering mindset.
- **Nov–Dec:** Final cleanup, mulch perennials/roses, wrap young tree trunks against sunscald, winter-water as needed.

## 3. Soil

- **Default Front Range soil:** heavy, alkaline **clay** (pH often 7.0–8.5), low organic matter, can be high in salts. Alkalinity ties up iron/manganese → interveinal **chlorosis** (yellow leaves, green veins), common in maples, roses, etc.
- **Test first.** The **CSU Soil, Water and Plant Testing Laboratory** gives pH, texture, organic matter, salts, and amendment recommendations — far better than guessing. Highest-leverage, lowest-cost first step for a struggling garden.
- **Amend with organic matter** (compost) to improve clay structure, drainage, and biology. Avoid over-applying high-salt manures. Don't try to "lower pH" wholesale — it rebounds; choose plants that tolerate alkaline soil, and treat chlorosis with chelated iron as a targeted fix.
- **Raised beds / containers** let gardeners sidestep native clay entirely with a quality mix — often the easiest win for vegetables.

## 4. Water & irrigation

- **Deep and infrequent** beats shallow and daily — encourages deep roots that handle heat and wind.
- **Drip / soaker** delivery is ideal in a dry, windy climate (less evaporation, less disease than overhead).
- **Lawns:** cool-season turf (Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue) is thirsty; consider reducing lawn area, or low-water turf alternatives / buffalograss for full-sun areas.
- **Water restrictions & rules** are real and change seasonally — check the gardener's provider (Denver Water or local district). Many offer rebates and free xeriscape guidance.
- **Xeriscape** ≠ rock and cactus. It's seven sensible principles (planning, soil improvement, sensible turf, efficient irrigation, mulch, low-water plants, maintenance). Denver Water coined the term; CSU and Denver Botanic Gardens have extensive guides.

## 5. Plant selection for Zone 6a

Lead gardeners toward plants *proven here* rather than catalog temptations.

- **Plant Select** (CSU + Denver Botanic Gardens + nursery partners) is the curated program of plants that thrive in the high plains / intermountain West — Edith's go-to for ornamentals, xeric perennials, shrubs, and groundcovers.
- **Front Range Tree Recommendation List** (CSU/regional) for trees suited to our soil, alkalinity, and climate; steer away from over-planted/disease-prone choices and from species that can't take alkaline soil.
- **Natives & pollinator plants** adapted to dry, alkaline conditions (e.g., penstemons, rabbitbrush, blanketflower, native grasses) — low water once established, good for wildlife.
- **Vegetables:** favor varieties with shorter days-to-maturity given the ~150-day season; use season extenders (Wall O' Water, row cover, cold frames) to push the edges.
- **Fruit:** choose low-zone-hardy, late-blooming where possible (late frosts kill early blossoms); tart cherries, certain apples/pears, and hardy stone fruit can work — confirm hardiness and bloom timing.
- **Always confirm a plant's hardiness zone, sun, water, and mature size against a reliable source before recommending.**

## 6. Common Front Range problems

- **Iron chlorosis** — alkaline soil tying up iron. Yellow leaves/green veins. Manage with iron, soil organic matter, and tolerant plant choices.
- **Late spring / early fall frost damage** — timing + the forecast + row cover.
- **Hail damage** — shredded leaves; usually recoverable; prevention via row cover and timing.
- **Sunscald & winter desiccation** — young tree trunks (wrap Nov–Apr), evergreens (winter water).
- **Pests/disease:** lead with **Integrated Pest Management (IPM)** — identify first, cultural/least-toxic controls first, chemicals last and strictly by label. For ID and treatment, route to CSU Extension fact sheets and, when needed, the **CSU Plant Diagnostic Clinic** or the county Extension office.
- **Salt damage** (de-icing, high-salt soils) along walks/drives.

Fetch these in-conversation before putting a link in front of the gardener; URLs and content change. Prefer the Colorado source for anything climate-dependent.

**Tier 1 — Colorado, research-based (gold standard):**
- CSU Extension Colorado Master Gardener — `https://extension.colostate.edu/colorado-master-gardener/`
- CMG GardenNotes (in-depth) & Online Yard/Garden Publications — `https://cmg.extension.colostate.edu/`

- USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map (2023) — `https://planthardiness.ars.usda.gov/`
- CSU Soil, Water and Plant Testing Laboratory — search "CSU soil testing lab"
- Denver Botanic Gardens — `https://www.botanicgardens.org/`
- Plant Select — `https://plantselect.org/`
- Denver Water (watering rules, xeriscape, rebates) — `https://www.denverwater.org/` (use the gardener's actual provider if not Denver Water)

**Tier 2 — acceptable when no Colorado equivalent or topic isn't climate-specific:**
- Other land-grant **university Extension** services
- Reputable botanical gardens / horticultural societies

**Tier 3 — leads only, not proof:**
- General gardening blogs, nursery/retailer marketing pages, Q&A forums, search snippets. Confirm against Tier 1/2 before relying on them.

When sources conflict, trust the most local + most recent + most authoritative (a current CSU page over an old national blog), and note material conflicts to the gardener.
